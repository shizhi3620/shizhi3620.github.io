A retrospective for a public project can easily collapse into outcomes: what shipped, what feedback arrived, and what might come next. The more useful part is whether people can understand and revisit the trade-offs made along the way.

I separate a retrospective into three layers. The first is verifiable fact, such as public commits, documentation, issue records, and release notes. The second is the judgment at the time: why one problem came first and another option was postponed. Only the third is personal feeling. It can clarify context, but it cannot stand in for evidence.

This approach helps in two ways. Collaborators get a project history rather than a collection of disconnected links. I get a record of the conditions behind a choice when facing similar constraints again, instead of relying on a vague memory of success or failure.

A retrospective does not need to promise that every question is answered. It is more honest to name assumptions that remain untested, state the current boundaries, and reduce the next step to an executable experiment or maintenance task.
